Moscow commands 100,000 men. They hold Kabul, the airports, and the concrete highways.
But they hold nothing else. 90% of Afghanistan belongs to the Mujahideen. The Holy Warriors.
They are not one army. They are dozens of rival tribes. They wear no uniforms.
They know every cave. They strike Soviet convoys at night and vanish into the rocks before dawn.
The Soviets fight back with total destruction. Mi-24 Hind helicopters bomb villages to dust.
They drop millions of tiny plastic mines disguised as toys, designed to blow off the feet of children.
Millions of Afghans flee to Pakistan. The villages are empty.
But in the Panjshir Valley, one man refuses to run.
Ahmad Shah Massoud. An engineering student turned brilliant guerrilla commander.
The Soviet Army launches nine massive offensives to kill him. Nine times they flood his valley with tanks.
Nine times Massoud retreats to the side-canyons, bleeds them dry, and forces them out.
The Afghans call him the Lion of Panjshir.
But the Mujahideen cannot fight helicopters with old rifles. They need money.
And they are about to get billions of it from a silent partner.
The CIA launches Operation Cyclone. It is the largest, most expensive covert operation in American history.
The goal is simple: give the Soviet Union their own Vietnam.
But America needs plausible deniability.
If Soviet soldiers are killed by American weapons, it could start World War Three.
So, the CIA buys hundreds of thousands of old Soviet rifles from Egypt and China.
They funnel the weapons entirely through Pakistan's intelligence agency, the ISI.
The Americans supply the guns, but the Pakistanis decide which warlords get them.
The CIA doesn't act alone. Saudi Arabia agrees to match American funding, dollar for dollar.
By the late 1980s, hundreds of millions of dollars a year are flooding into the mountains.
The money attracts thousands of foreign volunteers.
Radicalized young men from across the Arab world travel to Pakistan to join the Holy War against the atheists.
Among them is a wealthy, quiet young man from a prominent Saudi construction family.
He uses his fortune to build roads and tunnels for the fighters. His name is Osama Bin Laden.
By 1986, the Mujahideen are taking massive casualties.
The Soviet Hind helicopter is a flying tank. It is impervious to ground fire. It dominates the sky.
The CIA makes a desperate, highly controversial decision. They cross their own red line.
They decide to send the Mujahideen advanced American technology: The FIM-92 Stinger.
September 25, 1986. Jalalabad.
A group of Mujahideen hide in the brush with the new weapon.
A squad of Soviet helicopters approaches for a landing.
The fighter pulls the trigger. Within seconds, three Soviet helicopters are blown out of the sky.
The Stinger is a heat-seeking, shoulder-fired nightmare.
The dynamic of the war flips overnight. Soviet pilots are terrified.
They are forced to fly at extreme altitudes where they can't see their targets, or fly so low they crash.
The Soviets lose the sky.
Back in Moscow, the empire is rotting from the inside.
In 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev takes power. The economy is collapsing.
The war is costing billions of rubles they don't have.
Gorbachev calls Afghanistan a "bleeding wound." He knows the war is unwinnable.
But a superpower cannot simply run away without looking weak.
He tries to negotiate a peace. The Mujahideen refuse.
The human cost becomes impossible to hide. Fifteen thousand Soviet boys are dead.
They are shipped home in sealed, zinc-lined coffins.
In military slang, the dead are called "Cargo 200."
The Soviet government is so desperate to hide the scale of the disaster,
that families are forbidden from writing "Died in Afghanistan" on the gravestones of their own sons.
The survivors, known as the Afgantsy, return home with severe PTSD and heroin addictions.
They come back to a country that pretends their war never even happened.
By 1988, Moscow breaks. In Geneva, they sign an accord to withdraw.
The mighty Soviet Army, which defeated Nazi Germany, is ordered to pack up and leave in total defeat.
The retreat is a logistical nightmare.
For nine months, columns of armor drive north through the treacherous Salang Tunnel, constantly harassed by guerrilla fire.
And finally, on February 15, 1989, General Boris Gromov walks across the Friendship Bridge.
His 14-year-old son is waiting for him with flowers. The Soviet war is over.
Over a million Afghans are dead. The country is completely destroyed.
Moscow leaves behind a puppet government in Kabul, expecting it to fall in weeks.
Miraculously, it survives for three years.
But in December 1991, the Soviet Union itself collapses.
The money stops. The weapons stop. Within months, the Mujahideen storm into Kabul.
There is no peace. The warlords, suddenly without a common enemy, immediately turn their guns on each other.
A brutal civil war breaks out. They destroy Kabul faster than the Soviets ever did.
Out of this chaos and disgust, a new movement rises from the religious refugee schools in Pakistan.
They are extreme. They are ruthless. They call themselves the students. The Taliban.
By 1996, the Taliban capture Kabul.
They drag the former communist president out of a UN compound, murder him, and hang his body in the street.
Ahmad Shah Massoud, the Lion of Panjshir, retreats to the north.
He warns the world about the extremists gathering in his country.
On September 9, 2001, two assassins posing as journalists detonate a camera bomb. Massoud is killed.
The assassins were sent by Osama Bin Laden.
Two days later, Bin Laden's men fly commercial airplanes into the World Trade Center in New York.
Within weeks, a new foreign superpower arrives in Afghanistan. This time, from America.
They come to hunt Bin Laden and overthrow the Taliban. They expect a quick victory.
They stay for twenty years.
And in August 2021, the Americans leave, and the Taliban ride right back into Kabul.
The cycle of the graveyard completes itself.
The Soviet General who walked across the Friendship bridge came home to an empire that would not exist three years later.
The country he left behind is still bleeding today.
