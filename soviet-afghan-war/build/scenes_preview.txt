On February 15th, 1989, a Soviet general walks entirely alone across a steel bridge over the Amu Darya River.
He doesn't look back. Behind him, there is not a single Soviet soldier left in Afghanistan.
Nine years earlier, they rolled in with eighty thousand men.
They thought they would be there for a few months to guard some roads.
Instead, they walked into a trap that would destroy their entire empire.
This is the story of a superpower crushed by a phantom enemy.
This is the entire history of the Soviet-Afghan War.
The war does not start with an invasion. It starts with a murder.
In 1978, Afghan communists seize power in Kabul.
They want to modernize the country overnight. Land reform. Secular laws.
But Afghanistan is a nation of devout mountain tribes.
They see the communists as atheists trying to destroy Islam.
The countryside erupts into open rebellion.
The Afghan communist leader, Taraki, begs Moscow for troops. Moscow refuses.
The real danger to Taraki isn't the rebels. It's his own deputy: Hafizullah Amin.
In September 1979, on Amin's orders, Taraki is held down and smothered to death with a pillow.
The Kremlin is terrified.
They think Amin, who once studied in New York, might betray them and hand Afghanistan to the Americans.
The KGB decides Amin must die.
December 27, 1979. Amin is throwing a dinner party in his new palace on a hill in Kabul.
Suddenly, he and his guests collapse. The KGB has poisoned the soup.
But Soviet doctors, who don't know about the KGB plot, pump his stomach and save his life.
The KGB realizes the poison failed. So they send in the Spetsnaz.
The firefight lasts 40 minutes. Amin is shot dead in his underwear.
Moscow installs a puppet leader and sends in the 40th Army to secure the cities.
They think the job is done. It has just begun.
