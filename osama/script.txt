You know how Osama bin Laden died. But do you know why he hid for five years, less than a mile from Pakistan's top military academy… until one phone call he never even made?

This is the full story of the ten-year hunt. From the morning of 9/11 to forty minutes inside a house in Abbottabad. And it starts at 8:46 a.m.

September 11, 2001. New York City. 8:46 in the morning. American Airlines Flight 11 crashes into the North Tower of the World Trade Center. Seventeen minutes later, a second plane tears into the South Tower.

A third plane hits the Pentagon. The fourth crashes in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after its passengers fight back against the hijackers and bring it down.

In a single day, 2,977 people are killed. It is the deadliest terrorist attack ever on American soil.

That same day, in the mountains of eastern Afghanistan, Osama bin Laden sat with his men. He knew exactly what this attack was about to change. What he didn't know was that the longest, most frightening escape of his life had just begun.

The CIA, the FBI and the NSA moved without wasting a second. The first job: go through the passport and visa records of every passenger on all four hijacked flights. Every background was checked. Every travel history was pulled. Out of that data, 19 names stood out — the likely hijackers.

Then a coincidence cracked the case. Mohamed Atta, the lead hijacker, had a bag that missed his connecting flight. It was left behind at Boston's Logan Airport. Agents opened it, and what they found was a goldmine: flight manuals, instructions for the hijackers, and Atta's handwritten will.

That one bag pointed straight at al-Qaeda and Osama bin Laden. Investigators then traced the hijackers' old calls, emails and bank transfers. The money led straight back into al-Qaeda's network. The case was sealed. The target was clear: Osama bin Laden.

On October 7, 2001, America struck Afghanistan. Operation Enduring Freedom had begun. Within weeks, the Taliban regime fell apart. But by then, bin Laden had vanished into Tora Bora — a maze of mountains on the border between Afghanistan and Pakistan.

This was no ordinary mountain range. During the war against the Soviets, bin Laden and the mujahideen had dug deep tunnels and bunkers into these mountains. The terrain was so complex that without local knowledge, nobody could get in. Bin Laden knew every path.

The CIA's first clue came from an al-Qaeda member captured in Afghanistan. He said he had seen bin Laden heading toward Tora Bora. But one clue wasn't enough to launch an attack.

The second came from a team that monitored al-Qaeda's unsecured radio traffic. They noticed a strange pattern. Every day, someone was flashing codes with mirrors, from one peak to the next. It was an old al-Qaeda trick — signals that left no electronic trace.

Then one day, bin Laden slipped. He spoke over an unsecured radio, and the CIA caught the transmission. A specialist named Jalal, who had studied bin Laden's voice for seven years, listened and confirmed it at once: it's him, and he's in Tora Bora.

In December 2001, American and Northern Alliance forces began pounding Tora Bora. Bin Laden looked finished. Then a message came over the radio. He was ready to surrender — if they stopped the bombing and gave him a one-day ceasefire.

The CIA was suspicious. But they could hear him on the radio, telling his fighters to surrender. They fell for it. On December 11, the Northern Alliance was told to make the deal. The surrender was set for December 12.

But the moment the bombs stopped, bin Laden made his real move. He gathered his bodyguards, climbed onto horses and mules, and slipped away toward Pakistan in the dark. It later emerged that during the ceasefire, Pakistani tribal forces on the other side had let him through.

Tora Bora became one of the biggest intelligence failures in American history. Bin Laden was gone. And what he did next was the work of a mastermind.

The moment he reached Pakistan, bin Laden changed his entire communication system. No mobile phone. No satellite phone. No internet. Not even direct contact with his own al-Qaeda members. The man who wanted to hide from the whole world simply walked out of the digital world.

His only link to the outside was one man: Ibrahim al-Kuwaiti, known to the CIA as Abu Ahmed al-Kuwaiti — al-Qaeda's most trusted man. Bin Laden wrote letters by hand every day and signed them with a code name. Ibrahim carried them, as a courier, into al-Qaeda's network. The replies came back the same way, on handwritten paper.

For urgent messages only, there was a clever trick. Ibrahim would log into a Gmail account and write the message in the drafts folder. Other al-Qaeda members logged into the same account and read the draft. The message was never sent, so it left no digital trail. It was an old spy technique, and al-Qaeda borrowed it.

When the work became too much for one man, Ibrahim brought in his brother, Abrar al-Kuwaiti. Both became bin Laden's couriers. But two single men living alone would make people suspicious. So both were married — to women named Bushra and Mariam — so they would look like an ordinary Pashtun family.

Meanwhile, year after year, the CIA tried everything. In Pakistan's tribal areas, matchboxes were printed with a reward of up to $25 million for information on bin Laden. There were even claims of secret tracking chips planted in phones and computers sold in the region.

But none of it worked. Bin Laden had given up electronics completely.

In 2009, the CIA played another card. Humam al-Balawi, also known as Abu Dujana, was a Jordanian doctor arrested over his al-Qaeda links. Jordanian intelligence, working with the CIA, made him an offer: become our double agent, lead us to al-Qaeda's leaders, and walk free.

Balawi said yes. He was given brief training. Then word was spread that he had escaped.

Balawi won al-Qaeda's trust. He kept passing small pieces of inside information. He even claimed he had been asked to treat al-Qaeda's number two, Ayman al-Zawahiri. Everything seemed to be working.

Then he messaged the CIA on a secure network. I've learned something huge, he said — something that can get you to al-Qaeda's top leadership. I can't say it on the phone. I'll tell you in person.

The CIA was thrilled. On December 30, 2009, Balawi was invited to Camp Chapman in Afghanistan's Khost province, one of the CIA's most secure bases. It was his birthday that week, and the team had even arranged a surprise cake. Senior CIA officers were there to meet him.

Balawi arrived at the gate. At the first checkpoint, no search — the CIA had said not to. At the second, no search either. He walked straight in, stood among the officers, and in the next moment he blew himself up.

Seven CIA officers were killed, along with a Jordanian intelligence officer and an Afghan driver. It was the CIA's deadliest day in decades. Balawi had never been the CIA's double agent. He was al-Qaeda's triple agent, and the CIA itself was the target.

The hunt slowed down even more, and new CIA leadership had to step in. On the other side, bin Laden's own health was a worry. He was said to have kidney disease, and some reports claimed he needed dialysis. Al-Qaeda needed somewhere safe and permanent for him — and years earlier, they had already found it.

Abbottabad. Home of the Pakistan Military Academy at Kakul — Pakistan's West Point. Just a short distance away, in Bilal Town, a plot of land was up for sale. Al-Qaeda's logic was simple: nobody would imagine the world's most wanted man living right next to a military academy.

Abrar was sent with a fake ID and cash. He bought the 38,000-square-foot plot. A local architect, Mohammad Younis, was hired, and the design was unusual. Concrete walls up to 18 feet high, topped with barbed wire. No windows on the top floor. A 7-foot wall around the balcony so nobody could look in. And only one way in.

By 2006, the house was ready. Bin Laden, Ibrahim and Abrar moved in with their families. From the outside, nobody could tell. For five years, no one found out. Then, on August 27, 2010, one small mistake changed everything.

Ibrahim had to go to Peshawar for some work. Peshawar's entire mobile network was under CIA surveillance — and Ibrahim knew it. That's why, in Peshawar, he always used secure methods. But that day, he got careless. Standing in a public place, he used his personal phone and spoke to an al-Qaeda member for just a few seconds.

That was all it took. That was the phone call bin Laden never made — and it would cost him everything.

The CIA locked onto his live location. Agents reached the spot, but they didn't arrest him. They followed him. A white Suzuki jeep, with a rhino on the spare-tyre cover. Ibrahim drove it all the way to Bilal Town in Abbottabad, and through the gate of a large compound.

The agents waited outside. When they took a proper look at the compound, they were stunned. 38,000 square feet. 18-foot walls. Barbed wire. No windows upstairs. A 7-foot wall on the balcony.

At first, the CIA thought bin Laden couldn't be here. So close to a military academy? He'd never take that risk. Maybe this compound belonged to another high-value target — someone who could lead them to bin Laden.

Over the next weeks, the CIA watched closely. They contacted a retired Pakistani army officer, Lieutenant Colonel Syed Iqbal. His friend Dr. Amir Aziz lived just 80 yards from the compound. Iqbal visited Aziz, went up to the roof on the excuse of meeting the dog, and took several photos of the compound.

The CIA rented a place about a mile away and turned it into a secret base. Agents drove there every day in a quiet, unmarked car. Infrared and night-vision cameras went up. Listening devices were hidden in the trees nearby. Every movement, day and night, was recorded. When they ate. When they slept. When they came outside.

Slowly, a picture formed. Twenty to twenty-two people lived in the compound. And most importantly: every day, a tall man came out onto the top-floor balcony and walked quickly back and forth. Then he went inside. His face never showed — only his shadow on the balcony wall.

A CIA analyst team led by Gina Bennett measured that shadow against the wall and wrote a report. The man was somewhere between 5 feet 5 inches and 6 feet 8 inches tall. A huge range. But one thing was clear: he was the head of the compound. He never lifted a finger to help with any work.

The CIA drew up several plans to confirm it with DNA. One was to tunnel underground to the compound's sewage and take DNA from human waste. The CIA already had DNA from bin Laden's family — his father had married 22 times and had 56 children. But that much digging would tip off the people inside. The plan was dropped.

The second plan was Dr. Shakil Afridi. A covert CIA operative approached this local Abbottabad doctor about a hepatitis B vaccination drive for children. The real goal was to vaccinate the children inside the compound and collect their DNA.

Dr. Afridi was paid $55,000. He hired nurses and health workers from Ayub Medical College. The drive started in Nawansher and worked its way toward Bilal Town.

When they reached the compound's gate and rang the bell, Ibrahim came out and sent them all away. They tried again and again by phone. Nothing worked.

That plan failed too. Pakistan later jailed Dr. Afridi for 33 years — later cut to 23 — in a case widely seen as punishment for helping the CIA. He is still in prison.

While the CIA kept failing, another story was playing out inside the compound. Ibrahim had cancer. Abrar was depressed. After years of this life, both were exhausted. They went to bin Laden and said: we can't do this any longer.

Bin Laden listened. Then he said: fine. September 11, 2011 is the tenth anniversary of 9/11. Just stay until then. After that, buy yourselves a retirement home in Jeddah. I'll leave this place too, and we'll sell the compound.

That meant only a few months were left. The CIA sensed it too: if they didn't move soon, bin Laden could vanish again.

By December 2010, CIA analyst Gina Bennett and her team had prepared a very special document, called Sensitive Site Exploitation — SSE. It had a floor-by-floor layout of the compound. Where the stairs were. How many steps. Where the car was parked. The height of every wall, from every angle.

Every resident had a card: bin Laden, Ibrahim, Abrar — floor, height, schedule, everything. Gina Bennett was 100% sure. Bin Laden was there.

The next step was the President. CIA Director Leon Panetta, Admiral William McRaven, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs Admiral Mike Mullen, Vice Chairman General James Cartwright, and Michael Vickers, a senior Pentagon counterterrorism official, all gathered in the White House Situation Room. A 3D model of the compound, built with help from the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, was placed in front of President Obama.

There were three options. One: send B-2 stealth bombers and drop dozens of bombs. The whole compound, gone. But civilians could die, the DNA proof of bin Laden could be destroyed, ties with Pakistan could collapse, and America would face outrage around the world.

Two: a precise drone strike. But the chance of bin Laden escaping was high.

Three: Admiral McRaven's plan. Without telling Pakistan, a special team of Navy SEALs would enter the compound, identify bin Laden face to face, kill him, and slip out quietly.

Obama thought it over for a while. Then he said yes to the third option.

The mission was named Operation Neptune Spear. Neptune, for the SEALs' roots in the sea. Spear, for a precise and deadly strike. McRaven promised Obama that everything would be ready in three weeks.

For the team, Red Squadron of SEAL Team Six was chosen — some of the most dangerous, best-trained commandos in the world. The helicopters would be flown by pilots of the US Army's 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment, the Night Stalkers. Their speciality: flying into enemy territory at night, under the radar.

Twenty-four operators were chosen. None of them was told who the real target was. Only that there was an operation on the Afghanistan–Pakistan border.

Two MH-60 Black Hawk stealth helicopters were specially modified. A lower heat signature. Almost no sound. Nearly invisible to radar. Their design was so secret that it stayed classified. Three CH-47 Chinook helicopters were kept as backup.

Training began in North Carolina, then moved to a remote stretch of the Nevada desert, where a replica of the compound was built. The team rehearsed there dozens of times. Every room, every door, every staircase — memorised.

But there was one mistake. The training compound had low walls and chain-link fencing. The real compound had 18-foot concrete walls. That difference would cost them later.

There was a backup plan for everything. What if bin Laden surrendered? Under the laws of war, a man who surrenders cannot be killed. So a team of lawyers and interpreters waited on the aircraft carrier USS Carl Vinson in the Arabian Sea.

What if the SEALs got trapped in Pakistan? Two exit routes were ready — one toward the Indian border, the other toward Karachi, near where the Carl Vinson was stationed. Even the media team was prepared. What to say if bin Laden died. If he surrendered. If the mission failed. Every script was already written.

April 26, 2011: the whole team reached Bagram Airfield in Afghanistan. One day of rest. April 27: they moved to Jalalabad Air Base and waited for orders.

April 29, 2011, 8:20 in the morning: Obama gave the final go-ahead. The date was set for April 30. But that day, the weather turned — thick cloud and fog. The mission was moved to May 1, 2011.

May 1, 2011. In Washington, it was 1:22 in the afternoon. In Afghanistan, close to 11 at night. CIA Director Panetta and Admiral McRaven launched the operation. Obama was playing golf — on purpose, so no one would sense anything.

Around 2 p.m., he walked into the Situation Room. His whole national security team was there: Vice President Biden, Secretary of State Clinton, Defense Secretary Gates. Live video was streaming in from a drone overhead.

That night in Abbottabad, bin Laden finished dinner and went up to the top floor to sleep, with his wives and children. He had no idea.

At Jalalabad, one Chinook stayed behind as backup. The other four — two Chinooks carrying a quick reaction force, and the two Black Hawks — lifted off toward Pakistan at 11 p.m. Within 15 minutes, they crossed into Pakistani airspace, flying very low. Pakistan's radar caught nothing.

Around midnight, the two Chinooks set down in the hills with the quick reaction force. At 12:30, the two Black Hawks reached bin Laden's compound.

Chalk One: 12 SEAL commandos. Chalk Two: 12 more SEALs, plus a military dog named Cairo and an interpreter. The plan: Chalk One would hover over the compound while its SEALs roped down inside. Chalk Two would cover them.

But in the very first moments, everything went wrong. As Chalk One hovered over the compound, it got trapped in its own downwash — an aerodynamic effect called vortex ring state. The pilot lost control. The training compound had low walls. The real compound's 18-foot concrete walls changed how the air moved.

At the last second, the pilot kept his head and brought the helicopter down nose-first in a controlled crash. The SEALs climbed out. Nobody was hurt. But the helicopter's tail was stuck on the wall.

The plan had to change. The operation was no longer secret — anyone who saw that wreck the next day would understand. So the second Black Hawk landed and stayed, and the backup Chinook was called in.

At the same time, the power across Abbottabad went out. That, too, was said to be part of the plan.

An IT professional in Abbottabad, Sohaib Athar, was working late that night. He tweeted: “Helicopter hovering above Abbottabad at 1AM (is a rare event).” Without knowing it, he became the first person in the world to report the operation.

Inside, the SEALs blew open the first gate with C4 explosives and moved toward Ibrahim's quarters. As a SEAL approached the door, Ibrahim opened fire from behind it. In the return fire, Ibrahim was killed.

His wife, terrified, opened the door so the children wouldn't be hit. The children were hiding behind sacks. Everyone there was held in place.

They blew the second gate with C4 and entered the main house. Abrar was on the ground floor. The interpreter called out to him. Abrar stepped forward and was shot. His wife was hit as well.

On the first floor was bin Laden's son, Khalid. He fired wildly with an AK-47. The SEALs took him down too.

The top floor. Rob O'Neill and his team climbed up, wearing night-vision goggles. A tall man was standing in the hallway. Osama bin Laden.

His wife Amal rushed forward to protect him. In that instant, bin Laden pushed her toward the SEALs and stepped back. For a second, the team held back — they feared she might be wearing a suicide vest. She wasn't. O'Neill shot Amal in the leg.

1:10 a.m.

At 1:10 a.m., two bullets struck bin Laden in the forehead, above his left eye. Osama bin Laden was dead.

Is it him?

But the team couldn't be sure yet. SEAL Matt Bissonnette pulled back a bedsheet, cleaned bin Laden's face, took photos and sent them on. Fifteen minutes of waiting for confirmation. They had forgotten to bring a measuring tape, so they couldn't even measure his height.

The interpreter asked bin Laden's daughters, Sumaiya and Mariam. Mariam lied: “This is Abdullah bin Mohammed.” But Sumaiya told the truth: “This is my father, Osama.” Eleven-year-old Safia confirmed it too. So did his wife Khairiah.

A message reached McRaven in Jalalabad over a satellite phone: “Geronimo, EKIA.” Geronimo was the code name for bin Laden. EKIA meant enemy killed in action. In the White House Situation Room, Obama and his team heard it. One long breath. Ten years of hunting, over.

The backup Chinook arrived. All the women and children were gathered in the compound's vegetable garden. From bin Laden's room, the SEALs took hard drives, phones, cameras, DVDs and papers — everything.

The crashed helicopter was blown up with C4. Only the tail section, stuck on the wall, survived. Sohaib Athar tweeted about that blast too.

At 2:26 a.m., the two helicopters left Pakistan by different routes. Bin Laden's DNA samples were split between them, so that if one crashed, the other would survive. The whole operation had taken 40 minutes.

In Jalalabad, the DNA was tested against that of bin Laden's sister, who had died in a hospital in Boston. A 99.9% match. In bin Laden's pocket were 500 euros and a few phone numbers — probably kept in case he had to run.

On the USS Carl Vinson, bin Laden's body was washed according to Islamic rites, wrapped in a white sheet, placed in a weighted bag and lowered into the Arabian Sea — so there would never be a grave, and never a shrine.

In Pakistan, it was 3:45 in the morning when the army reached the compound. Bin Laden's wives and children were taken to hospital in a curtained bus. Under questioning, they said they had lived there for five years, and described what happened that night.

At 11:35 p.m. Washington time, President Obama told the world.

When the CIA released the files from bin Laden's room, they revealed al-Qaeda's future plans. Documentaries about bin Laden himself, which he had ordered in to watch. Video games for the children. And adult content too.

After the operation, the team was praised and met President Obama. And Obama made a little joke: he gave Admiral McRaven a tape measure — because the team had forgotten to measure bin Laden's height.

Ten years. Countless mistakes. He slipped through their hands again and again. Then one courier, one careless phone call, and a 40-minute operation ended the story of the world's most wanted man.
