# The Entire History of Israel in 30 Minutes

## The Hook

On the eastern shore of the Mediterranean Sea, there is a strip of land so small that you can drive across its narrowest part in about twenty minutes.

Israel today is roughly the size of the American state of New Jersey. About ten million people live there. On a world map, you can cover it with your thumb.

And yet, almost no place on Earth has been fought over for so long, by so many empires, in the name of so many gods.

Egyptians, Assyrians, Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, Romans, Arabs, Crusaders, Mamluks, Ottomans and the British. Every one of them came, conquered, and eventually left.

Three of the world's great religions call this land holy. And in the middle of it sits one small city, Jerusalem, where a Jewish wall, a Christian church and a Muslim shrine stand within a few minutes' walk of each other.

So how did a people that was scattered across the world for almost two thousand years come back and build a country here? Why did the very same moment mean independence for one people, and catastrophe for another? And why, after more than seventy-five years, many wars and dozens of peace plans, is the story still not over?

Here is the key, and it runs through this entire story. This is not the story of one people and one land. It is the story of two peoples, with deep roots in the same small land, each convinced that it belongs to them. Keep that in mind, because everything else follows from it.

Let's go back to the very beginning.

## Part 1: The Ancient Kingdom

Around three thousand years ago, the hills between the Mediterranean and the Jordan River were home to a group of tribes called the Israelites.

According to the Hebrew Bible, their story began with Abraham, a man who left Mesopotamia and was promised this land for his descendants. Historians can't confirm those early stories. But we do know that by around 1000 BCE, the Israelites had built small kingdoms in these hills.

Tradition says the greatest king was David, who made Jerusalem his capital. His son Solomon then built a great temple there, the First Temple, the holiest place of the Jewish faith.

After Solomon, the kingdom split in two. Israel in the north, and Judah in the south, with Jerusalem. And that is where the word Jew comes from. A Jew was originally a person from Judah.

Being small, between giants, was dangerous. In 722 BCE, the Assyrian Empire wiped out the northern kingdom and deported its people. Then in 586 BCE, the Babylonians came for Judah. They burned Jerusalem, destroyed the First Temple and marched thousands of people into exile in Babylon.

Fifty years later, the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon and did something unusual. He let the exiles go home. They returned to Jerusalem and built a Second Temple.

Over the next centuries, the land passed from Persians to Greeks. When a Greek king tried to ban Jewish worship, a family called the Maccabees led a revolt and won. Jews still celebrate that victory every year as Hanukkah.

Then came Rome. In 63 BCE, the Roman general Pompey marched into Jerusalem, and Judea became part of the Roman world. Rome installed King Herod, who rebuilt the Temple on a gigantic platform. One wall of that platform still stands today. We will come back to it.

## Part 2: Two Thousand Years Away

Roman rule was harsh, and in the year 66, the Jews of Judea rebelled. Rome answered with overwhelming force. In 70 CE, Roman legions captured Jerusalem and destroyed the Second Temple. It was never rebuilt.

Sixty years later, a second revolt broke out, led by a man named Bar Kokhba. This time, Rome did not just win. It tried to erase the memory of the place. Jerusalem was rebuilt as a Roman city, Jews were banned from living there, and the province was renamed Syria Palaestina, after the ancient Philistines.

That is where the name Palestine comes from.

From this point, most Jews lived outside the land. In Babylon, in North Africa, in Spain, and later across Europe. This scattering is called the diaspora. Small Jewish communities stayed in the land, but for most Jews, it became a memory and a prayer. Every year, at the end of the Passover meal, Jews around the world said the same words. Next year in Jerusalem.

Meanwhile, the land itself moved on. It became Christian under the Byzantine Empire. Then in the seventh century, Arab Muslim armies arrived, and Jerusalem surrendered to the Caliph Umar.

In 691, the Muslims built the Dome of the Rock on top of the old Temple platform, at the spot where Islamic tradition says the Prophet Muhammad ascended to heaven. Jerusalem became the third holiest city in Islam.

Over the next centuries, the population gradually became mostly Arabic-speaking and mostly Muslim, with Christian and Jewish minorities. These are the ancestors of most of the people we now call Palestinians.

Then the Crusaders came from Europe and took Jerusalem in 1099. Saladin took it back in 1187. The Mamluks of Egypt ruled after him. And in 1517, the land fell to the Ottoman Turks, who would rule it for the next four hundred years.

And here is something people often forget. When Spain expelled all its Jews in 1492, it was the Ottoman sultan who welcomed them. For centuries, Jews in the Muslim world mostly lived as a protected minority, while in Christian Europe they were repeatedly expelled, massacred and locked into ghettos.

It was Europe, not the Middle East, that would set the next chapter in motion.

## Part 3: A Dream Called Zionism

By the late 1800s, millions of Jews lived in the Russian Empire, and life there was getting worse. After Tsar Alexander the Second was assassinated in 1881, waves of violent attacks called pogroms swept through Jewish towns.

Even in modern, liberal France, things were not safe. In 1894, a Jewish army officer named Alfred Dreyfus was falsely convicted of spying for Germany. Crowds in Paris shouted death to the Jews.

A journalist from Vienna named Theodor Herzl watched all of this. And he came to a radical conclusion. Jews would never be safe as a minority in someone else's country. They needed a state of their own.

In 1896, he published a short book called The Jewish State. A year later, he gathered about two hundred delegates in Basel, Switzerland, for the First Zionist Congress. The movement took its name from Zion, an ancient name for Jerusalem.

That night, Herzl wrote in his diary: at Basel, I founded the Jewish state. If I said this out loud today, everyone would laugh. But in fifty years, everyone will see it.

Remember that number. Fifty years.

Jewish immigrants began arriving in Ottoman Palestine. They bought land, drained swamps and built farming villages. In 1909, a group of families founded a new town on the sand dunes north of the old port of Jaffa. They called it Tel Aviv. A year later came the first kibbutz, a village where everything was shared.

But the land was not empty. Around 1914, roughly seven hundred thousand people lived in Ottoman Palestine, and about nine out of ten of them were Arabs. They had their own towns, farms and families going back centuries. And many of them began to watch the newcomers with growing worry.

Two national movements were now growing in the same small land. And soon, a world war would make promises to both of them.

## Part 4: One Land, Two Promises

In 1914, the Ottoman Empire joined the First World War on Germany's side. Britain wanted to break it apart, and it started making promises.

In 1915, a British official named Henry McMahon wrote letters to Hussein, the Sharif of Mecca. If the Arabs rebelled against the Ottomans, Britain would support Arab independence. The Arabs believed that included Palestine.

In 1916, Britain and France secretly signed the Sykes-Picot Agreement, dividing the Middle East between themselves.

And in November 1917, the British Foreign Secretary, Arthur Balfour, wrote a letter to Lord Rothschild, a leader of the British Jewish community. Britain, it said, viewed with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people.

The same letter added that nothing should be done to harm the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. That was about ninety percent of the population, and they were not asked.

One land. Three promises. You can probably guess how this goes.

A month later, British troops marched into Jerusalem. Four hundred years of Ottoman rule were over. After the war, the League of Nations handed Palestine to Britain to govern, as a Mandate. The land east of the Jordan River was split off as a separate Arab territory, Transjordan, which later became the Kingdom of Jordan.

Under British rule, Jewish immigration continued, and so did the clashes. In 1929, riots broke out, and in the ancient city of Hebron, Arab rioters killed sixty-seven Jews.

Then in 1933, Adolf Hitler came to power in Germany. Jews tried desperately to escape, and many countries closed their doors. Tens of thousands went to Palestine. In just a few years, the Jewish population more than doubled.

For the Arabs, it looked like they were becoming a minority in their own land. In 1936, they launched a great revolt against British rule and Jewish immigration. Britain crushed it with force, but it also got the message.

In 1937, a British commission suggested something new. Cut the land in two. A small Jewish state, a larger Arab state. The idea of partition was born. It went nowhere. And in 1939, Britain, needing Arab support for the coming war, sharply limited Jewish immigration.

The timing could not have been worse.

## Part 5: The Darkest Chapter

During the Second World War, Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered six million Jews in the Holocaust. Around two out of every three Jews in Europe were killed. Entire communities that had existed for a thousand years simply disappeared.

When the war ended, hundreds of thousands of survivors were living in camps for displaced people. Many of them had no homes to go back to. And many wanted only one thing: to go to Palestine.

Britain still said no. Ships packed with refugees were stopped at sea. In 1947, the ship Exodus arrived carrying more than four thousand survivors, and the British sent them back to Europe. The world was watching, and it was a public relations disaster.

At the same time, Jewish underground groups began attacking the British. In 1946, the group called the Irgun bombed the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, the headquarters of the British administration. Ninety-one people were killed.

Britain was exhausted, broke, and caught between two peoples who both wanted it gone. So in 1947, it handed the whole problem to the brand-new United Nations.

## Part 6: The Partition

The UN sent a special committee to Palestine. Its majority came back with a plan. Split the land into two states, one Jewish and one Arab, with Jerusalem under international control.

On November 29th, 1947, the UN General Assembly voted. Thirty-three countries in favour, thirteen against, ten abstentions. The plan passed.

Under the plan, the Jewish state would get about fifty-six percent of the land, much of it the Negev desert. The Arab state would get about forty-three percent.

The Jewish leadership accepted it. For them, after the Holocaust, any state was a lifeline.

The Arab leadership rejected it. Arabs were about two thirds of the population, and Jews owned only around seven percent of the land. To them, it made no sense that a UN vote should hand more than half of their country to a minority that had mostly arrived within living memory.

Both sides believed they were right. And within days, fighting broke out.

It began as a civil war between Jewish and Arab militias, village against village, road against road. Both sides committed atrocities. In April 1948, Jewish fighters killed more than a hundred villagers in the Arab village of Deir Yassin. Days later, Arab fighters ambushed a Jewish medical convoy heading to a Jerusalem hospital and killed seventy-eight people.

News of Deir Yassin spread fast, and fear spread with it. Arab families began to flee their homes.

And on May 14th, 1948, the last British soldiers prepared to leave.

## Part 7: 1948

That afternoon, in a small museum in Tel Aviv, a sixty-one-year-old man named David Ben-Gurion stood up and read a declaration. The Jewish state in the land of Israel would be called the State of Israel.

Fifty years and a few months after Herzl wrote in his diary that in fifty years everyone would see it.

Eleven minutes later, the United States recognised the new country. The Soviet Union followed three days after that.

But the next morning, the armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria and Iraq, together with forces from Lebanon and volunteers from across the Arab world, invaded. Their goal was to stop the new state before it could take root.

Israel was outnumbered at first, but its forces were better organised, and as the war went on they received weapons from abroad. By early 1949, when the fighting ended, Israel controlled about seventy-eight percent of the former Mandate, much more than the UN plan had offered.

The rest was split up. Jordan took the West Bank and East Jerusalem, including the Old City. Egypt took a narrow strip of coast around the town of Gaza. The Arab state from the UN plan was never created.

The ceasefire lines drawn in 1949 became known as the Green Line, because they were drawn in green ink on the map.

For Israelis, 1948 is the War of Independence. A miracle. A people that had nearly been destroyed now had a country.

For Palestinians, 1948 is the Nakba. The catastrophe. About seven hundred thousand Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled from their homes during the war. Some ran from the fighting. Others were driven out by Israeli forces. More than four hundred Arab villages were emptied and most were later destroyed.

Israel did not allow the refugees to return. They ended up in camps in Gaza, the West Bank, Jordan, Lebanon and Syria. Today, their descendants number in the millions, and many still keep the keys to houses that no longer exist.

And there is another exodus that is often forgotten. Over the next two decades, around eight hundred thousand Jews left or were driven out of Arab and Muslim countries, from Iraq and Yemen to Morocco and Egypt. Most of them moved to Israel.

Two peoples. Two stories. The same year.

## Part 8: A Country of Immigrants

The new state was poor, and it was flooded with newcomers. Holocaust survivors from Europe, Jews from Iraq, Yemen and Morocco. In its first three years, Israel's Jewish population doubled. People lived in tents and food was rationed.

In 1956, Israel joined Britain and France in a secret plan to seize the Suez Canal from Egypt. Militarily, it worked. Politically, it was a disaster. The United States and the Soviet Union forced all three to pull back.

Egypt's president, Gamal Abdel Nasser, came out of it a hero of the Arab world. And he made it clear that the conflict with Israel was not over.

Meanwhile, the Palestinians were organising. In 1964, the Palestine Liberation Organization, the PLO, was founded. A few years later, a young engineer named Yasser Arafat became its leader, and Palestinian armed groups began launching attacks on Israel.

By 1967, the region was a powder keg.

## Part 9: Six Days

In May 1967, Nasser moved tens of thousands of troops into the Sinai Peninsula, ordered UN peacekeepers out, and closed the Straits of Tiran to Israeli ships. Arab radio stations promised that Israel would soon be destroyed.

Israelis were terrified. Some dug mass graves in public parks, just in case.

On the morning of June 5th, 1967, Israel struck first. In a few hours, its air force destroyed most of Egypt's warplanes while they were still on the ground. Then Jordan and Syria entered the war.

What followed lasted just six days.

Israel took the Gaza Strip and the whole Sinai Peninsula from Egypt. It took the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan. And it took the Golan Heights from Syria.

In those six days, the territory under Israel's control more than tripled.

On the third day, Israeli paratroopers reached the Old City of Jerusalem and the Western Wall. Remember Herod's Temple platform? That wall is what remained of it. For the first time in nineteen years, Jews could pray there.

For Israel, it was a stunning victory. For the Arab world, it was a humiliation known as the Naksa, the setback. Around three hundred thousand more Palestinians fled, many of them for the second time.

And now Israel ruled over about a million Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza. Without giving them citizenship, and without giving the land back.

The UN passed Resolution 242, which became the basis of almost every peace plan since. Israel would withdraw from occupied territories, and in return, its neighbours would recognise its right to live in peace. Land for peace.

But that same year, Arab leaders meeting in Khartoum announced three no's. No peace with Israel. No recognition of Israel. No negotiations with Israel.

And on the Israeli side, the first settlements began to appear in the occupied land.

## Part 10: The Day of Atonement

After 1967, many Israelis believed their army could never be beaten. That confidence would be tested.

On October 6th, 1973, on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the Jewish year, when much of the country was in synagogues, Egypt and Syria attacked at the same time.

Egyptian troops crossed the Suez Canal and broke through Israel's defensive line. Syrian tanks rolled into the Golan Heights. For a few days, Israel's leaders feared the worst.

Then Israel recovered. Its forces pushed the Syrians back, crossed the Suez Canal themselves and surrounded an Egyptian army. After almost three weeks, a ceasefire was agreed.

Israel had won again on the battlefield, but at a terrible cost. More than two thousand six hundred Israeli soldiers were killed, along with many thousands on the Egyptian and Syrian side. Arab oil producers cut supplies to the West, and petrol prices around the world exploded.

Prime Minister Golda Meir resigned the next year. And on both sides, people started to wonder whether war could ever solve this.

## Part 11: The Handshake in the Desert

Then came one of the biggest surprises of the century.

In November 1977, the president of Egypt, Anwar Sadat, the man who had launched the Yom Kippur war, flew to Jerusalem. He stood in Israel's parliament, the Knesset, and spoke of peace.

A year later, US President Jimmy Carter brought Sadat and Israel's prime minister, Menachem Begin, to Camp David, near Washington. For thirteen days they argued, walked out and came back. And in the end, they made a deal.

In 1979, Egypt and Israel signed a peace treaty. Israel gave the entire Sinai Peninsula back to Egypt. Egypt became the first Arab country to recognise Israel. That peace has held ever since.

But many in the Arab world saw it as a betrayal. In 1981, Sadat was assassinated by Islamist soldiers during a military parade.

Land for peace had worked once. With Egypt. But the Palestinian question was still unsolved.

## Part 12: Stones and Uprisings

In 1982, Israel invaded Lebanon, where the PLO had built its base. Israeli forces reached Beirut, and the PLO was forced to leave for Tunisia.

But the war turned into a long and bitter occupation of southern Lebanon. In September 1982, a Lebanese Christian militia allied with Israel massacred hundreds of Palestinian civilians in the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps. An Israeli inquiry found that the defence minister, Ariel Sharon, bore personal responsibility for failing to prevent it, and he had to resign.

And in southern Lebanon, a new Shia militia grew up fighting the Israeli occupation. It was called Hezbollah.

Then in December 1987, the Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza rose up themselves. Strikes, boycotts, protests, and above all young people throwing stones at Israeli soldiers. It was called the Intifada, the shaking off.

Television brought the images to the whole world. Heavily armed soldiers facing teenagers with stones.

That same month, in Gaza, a new Islamist movement was founded. It rejected any compromise with Israel. It was called Hamas.

## Part 13: Oslo

By the early 1990s, both sides were tired. And in secret, in Oslo, Norway, Israeli and Palestinian negotiators began to talk.

On September 13th, 1993, on the White House lawn, Israel's prime minister Yitzhak Rabin, a former general, shook hands with Yasser Arafat, the man Israel had called a terrorist for decades. US President Bill Clinton stood between them, with his arms open.

The Oslo Accords created a Palestinian Authority with limited self-rule in parts of the West Bank and Gaza. The hardest questions were left for later: borders, Jerusalem, refugees and settlements.

A year later, Jordan signed a peace treaty with Israel. Rabin, his foreign minister Shimon Peres and Arafat shared the Nobel Peace Prize.

But not everyone wanted this peace. In 1994, an Israeli extremist opened fire on Muslim worshippers in Hebron and killed twenty-nine people. Hamas answered with suicide bombings on Israeli buses.

And on November 4th, 1995, after a huge peace rally in Tel Aviv, Yitzhak Rabin was shot dead. Not by a Palestinian, but by a young Israeli extremist who wanted to stop the peace process.

The peace process never truly recovered.

In the summer of 2000, Clinton brought Arafat and Israel's prime minister Ehud Barak to Camp David to finish the job. They failed, and each side blamed the other.

Two months later, the Second Intifada began. This time it was far deadlier: suicide bombings in Israeli cafés and buses, and Israeli military operations in Palestinian cities. In about five years, roughly a thousand Israelis and more than three thousand Palestinians were killed.

Israel began building a huge separation barrier, walls and fences, in and around the West Bank. Israelis said it stopped the bombers. Palestinians said it grabbed their land and cut their communities apart.

## Part 14: Gaza

In 2005, Israel did something unexpected. It pulled all its soldiers and all eight thousand settlers out of the Gaza Strip.

A few months later, in 2006, Hamas won the Palestinian elections. A year after that, in a short and violent conflict, Hamas threw its rival Fatah out of Gaza and took full control.

Israel and Egypt answered with a blockade of the Gaza Strip, controlling what and who could go in and out. Israel said it was needed to stop weapons. Critics called Gaza the world's largest open-air prison. More than two million people were squeezed into a strip of land about forty kilometres long.

What followed was a cycle. Rockets from Gaza. Israeli airstrikes. Wars in 2008, 2012, 2014 and 2021. Each time, a ceasefire. Each time, nothing really solved.

Israel built Iron Dome, a system that shoots rockets out of the sky. And in the West Bank, the settlements kept growing. Today more than seven hundred thousand Israelis live in the West Bank and East Jerusalem, in settlements that most of the world considers illegal under international law. Israel disputes that.

Meanwhile, the region was changing. In 2020, the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain and Morocco signed normalisation deals with Israel, called the Abraham Accords. Saudi Arabia was talking about joining. Many began to believe the Palestinian question could simply be left behind.

They were wrong.

## Part 15: October 7th

On October 7th, 2023, thousands of Hamas fighters broke through the fence around Gaza and attacked Israeli towns, army posts and a music festival. About twelve hundred people were killed, most of them civilians. Around two hundred and fifty people were taken back to Gaza as hostages.

It was the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust.

Israel declared war on Hamas and launched a massive air and ground campaign in Gaza. What followed was the most destructive war in the history of the conflict.

According to the Gaza Health Ministry, which is run by Hamas, more than sixty-seven thousand Palestinians were killed in two years, and the UN human rights office found that most of the victims it could verify were women and children. Most of Gaza's two million people were forced from their homes, again and again, and much of the Strip was reduced to rubble. UN-backed experts declared famine in parts of Gaza.

Israel said it was fighting a group that hid among civilians and held its hostages in tunnels. South Africa accused Israel of genocide at the International Court of Justice, a charge Israel strongly rejects.

The war spread. Hezbollah fired from Lebanon, and Israel struck back hard. Israel and Iran, enemies for decades, attacked each other directly for the first time.

In October 2025, after two years of war, a ceasefire took hold, and the last living hostages came home. But Gaza lay in ruins, and as this video is being made, no one can say what comes next.

## The Ending

So let's go back to our questions.

How did a scattered people come back and build a country? Through an idea, Zionism, that was laughed at in 1897, through the catastrophe of the Holocaust, which convinced much of the world that Jews needed a state, and through winning war after war against bigger neighbours.

And why did the same moment mean catastrophe for another people? Because the land was not empty. The Palestinians had lived there for centuries, and the birth of Israel meant the loss of their homes and their hope of a state of their own. A hope they still have not achieved.

Remember the key. Two peoples, with deep roots in the same small land, each convinced that it belongs to them.

Every war, every peace deal, every map in this story was an attempt to answer one question. How do you share a land that both sides call home?

Herzl said that in fifty years, everyone would see his state. He was off by less than a year.

The question that nobody has answered yet, after more than seventy-five years, is whether two peoples can ever see each other.
