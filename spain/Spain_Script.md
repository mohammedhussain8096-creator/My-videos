# The Entire History of Spain in 30 Minutes

## The Hook

At the south-western corner of Europe, there is a peninsula so close to Africa that on a clear day, you can see one continent from the other. At its narrowest point, the gap is only fourteen kilometres of sea.

Spain covers most of that peninsula. Today, it is home to about forty-nine million people, and it is one of the most visited countries on Earth.

And yet, almost every great power of the ancient and medieval world wanted this land. Phoenicians, Greeks, Carthaginians, Romans, Visigoths, Arabs and Berbers. Every one of them came, and every one of them left something behind.

Then something strange happened. This corner of Europe, invaded by everyone, turned around and conquered half the world. For a time, it ruled the first empire on which the sun never set.

So how did a land that was conquered again and again become the most powerful empire on Earth? Why did the richest empire in history keep going bankrupt? And how did a country that tore itself apart in a brutal civil war become a modern democracy in a single generation?

Here is the key, and it runs through this entire story. Spain was never one thing. It was always many peoples and many kingdoms, with different languages and different faiths, sharing one peninsula. Every chapter of its history is a struggle between unity and difference. Keep that in mind, because everything else follows from it.

Let's go back to the very beginning.

## Part 1: The Edge of the World

Long before there was a Spain, there were people here. Deep inside a cave called Altamira, in the north, hunters painted bison on the ceiling, in red and black. Some of those paintings are more than thirty thousand years old.

By three thousand years ago, the peninsula was home to many different peoples. In the east and south lived the Iberians, who gave the peninsula its name. In the north and west lived Celtic tribes. And in the green mountains of the north lived the ancestors of the Basques, whose language is not related to any other language on Earth.

Then the traders arrived by sea. Around three thousand years ago, the Phoenicians, from what is now Lebanon, sailed all the way across the Mediterranean and founded a trading post called Gadir. Today we call it Cádiz, and it is one of the oldest cities in Western Europe.

The Greeks came next, and built their own colonies on the eastern coast. What attracted everyone was the same thing. Spain was rich in silver, copper and tin.

Then came Carthage, the great Phoenician city in North Africa. The Carthaginians built a new capital on the Spanish coast and called it New Carthage. Today, it is still called Cartagena.

And from here, in 218 BCE, a young Carthaginian general named Hannibal set out with an army and around thirty-seven elephants, to cross the Alps and attack Rome itself.

It was a brilliant gamble. But it brought Rome to Spain.

## Part 2: Hispania

Rome won the war against Carthage, and it took Spain as the prize. But conquering it was another matter.

The tribes of the interior fought back for two hundred years. In 133 BCE, the Celtiberian town of Numantia was besieged until its people starved, and many of them chose death rather than surrender. It was not until 19 BCE, under the Emperor Augustus, that the last tribes of the north were finally defeated.

The Romans called the land Hispania. And over the next four centuries, it became one of the richest parts of the empire.

The Romans built roads, bridges and aqueducts. The aqueduct of Segovia still stands, without a drop of mortar holding its stones together. They built cities like Mérida, Tarragona and Zaragoza. And the people of Hispania began to speak Latin, the language that would one day grow into Spanish, Catalan, Galician and Portuguese.

Hispania gave Rome more than silver and olive oil. The philosopher Seneca was born in Córdoba. Two of Rome's greatest emperors, Trajan and Hadrian, came from a town near Seville.

Christianity arrived too, and slowly spread through the cities.

But in the fifth century, the Roman Empire in the west collapsed. Germanic peoples crossed the Pyrenees. And the strongest of them, the Visigoths, made Hispania their kingdom, with its capital at Toledo.

For the first time, most of the peninsula was ruled by one king, from one city, in one Christian faith. It lasted about two hundred years.

## Part 3: Al-Andalus

In the year 711, a Berber commander named Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the narrow strait from Africa with an army of a few thousand men. He landed beside a great rock, which has carried his name ever since. Jabal Tariq, the mountain of Tariq. Gibraltar.

The Visigothic kingdom was divided by a civil war, and its last king, Roderic, was killed in battle. In less than ten years, almost the whole peninsula was under Muslim rule.

Almost. In the far north, in the mountains of Asturias, a Christian noble named Pelayo defeated a small Muslim force at Covadonga, around 722. It was a tiny battle. But Spanish tradition would later see it as the beginning of everything.

The Muslims called their new land Al-Andalus. And in 756, a young prince arrived who would change its history. His name was Abd al-Rahman. He was the last survivor of the Umayyad family, which had been massacred by its rivals in Syria. He fled all the way across North Africa, landed in Spain, and made himself ruler of Al-Andalus, with his capital in Córdoba.

In 929, his descendant declared himself caliph, a ruler equal to any in the Muslim world.

Córdoba became one of the greatest cities in Europe. It had paved streets, street lamps, public baths and libraries, at a time when most of Europe's towns were small and poor. Its great mosque, with its forest of red and white arches, still stands today.

Muslims, Christians and Jews lived side by side, though not as equals. Christians and Jews were protected minorities who paid a special tax. But scholars of all three faiths worked here, and through Spain, the lost knowledge of the ancient Greeks, in philosophy, mathematics and medicine, flowed back into Christian Europe.

Then, in 1031, the caliphate collapsed. Al-Andalus broke into more than twenty small kingdoms, called taifas, which fought one another.

And in the north, the Christian kingdoms were waiting.

## Part 4: The Reconquista

The small Christian territories of the north had grown into kingdoms. Leon and Castile. Navarre. Aragon. And on the western coast, Portugal. They fought the Muslims, but they also fought each other, and sometimes allied with Muslim rulers against their Christian rivals.

In 1085, King Alfonso the Sixth of Castile captured Toledo, the old Visigothic capital. Frightened, the taifa kings called for help from North Africa, and a fierce Berber dynasty, the Almoravids, crossed into Spain and stopped the Christian advance.

This was the age of El Cid, a Castilian knight who fought for Christian and Muslim lords alike, and who captured the city of Valencia in 1094. He became Spain's great national hero.

For more than a century, the frontier moved back and forth. Then, in 1212, the Christian kingdoms joined forces and crushed the Almohad army, another North African dynasty, at the battle of Las Navas de Tolosa.

After that, the great cities fell one by one. Córdoba in 1236. Valencia in 1238. Seville in 1248.

Only one Muslim kingdom survived, in the mountains of the south. Granada. For two and a half more centuries, its rulers paid tribute to Castile and built one of the most beautiful palaces in the world, the Alhambra.

Spaniards later called these centuries the Reconquista, the reconquest. But by now, the peninsula was divided into a few big Christian kingdoms, each with its own laws and its own language. Castile, the largest, in the centre. Aragon, with Catalonia and Valencia, in the east. Portugal in the west.

What would join them together was not a war. It was a wedding.

## Part 5: 1492

In 1469, two teenagers married in secret. Isabella, the heir to the throne of Castile, was eighteen. Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of Aragon, was seventeen.

Within ten years, both were on their thrones. They became known as the Catholic Monarchs. Their two kingdoms kept their own laws and parliaments, but for the first time, one couple ruled most of Spain.

They wanted one faith as well as one crown. In 1478, they set up the Spanish Inquisition, a church court to hunt down people whose conversion to Christianity was suspected to be false. Over the following centuries, it would put thousands of people to death.

And then came one single year that changed everything.

On the second of January 1492, after a ten-year war, the last Muslim king of Granada, Boabdil, handed the keys of the city to Ferdinand and Isabella. Seven hundred and eighty-one years after Tariq crossed the strait, Muslim rule in Spain was over.

Three months later, the monarchs ordered every Jew in their kingdoms to convert to Christianity or leave. Jews had lived in Spain for more than a thousand years. Tens of thousands left, many of them to North Africa and to the Ottoman Empire. Their descendants are still called Sephardic Jews, from Sefarad, the Hebrew name for Spain.

And in August of that same year, an Italian sailor named Christopher Columbus, paid by Queen Isabella, set out west with three small ships, looking for a sea route to Asia. On the twelfth of October 1492, he reached an island in the Caribbean. He thought he had arrived in the Indies.

He had found a world that Europe did not know existed.

The end of Muslim Spain. The expulsion of the Jews. And the discovery of the Americas. All in a single year.

Remember that year. 1492.

## Part 6: Gold and Empire

Two years later, Spain and Portugal divided the newly discovered world between them, with a line drawn down a map in the Treaty of Tordesillas. Everything to the west would be Spanish. That is why Brazil speaks Portuguese, and most of the rest of Latin America speaks Spanish.

Then came the conquistadors.

In 1519, Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico with around five hundred men. Two years later, with the help of tens of thousands of local allies who hated Aztec rule, he destroyed the Aztec capital, Tenochtitlan, one of the largest cities in the world. Mexico City was built on its ruins.

In 1532, Francisco Pizarro, with fewer than two hundred men, captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa in Peru. The emperor offered a room full of gold for his freedom. Pizarro took the gold, and executed him anyway.

How could so few men topple such great empires? Steel, horses and guns helped. Local allies helped even more. But the deadliest weapon was one the Spaniards did not even understand. They brought diseases like smallpox, to which the people of the Americas had no immunity. In the century after the conquest, the native population collapsed. In some regions, nine out of every ten people died.

Then the silver began to flow. In 1545, the Spanish found a mountain of silver at Potosí, in what is now Bolivia. Indigenous workers were forced to mine it under brutal conditions. Fleets of treasure ships carried the silver across the Atlantic, year after year.

And in 1522, one battered ship returned to Spain with eighteen exhausted men on board. The expedition had left three years earlier under Ferdinand Magellan, who was killed in the Philippines. Under the Basque captain Juan Sebastián Elcano, the survivors had done something no one had ever done before.

They had sailed all the way around the world.

## Part 7: The Empire Where the Sun Never Set

Isabella and Ferdinand's daughter married into the Habsburg family. And in 1516, their grandson Charles inherited it all. Castile, Aragon, Naples, Sicily, the Netherlands, and the Americas.

Three years later, he was also elected Holy Roman Emperor, as Charles the Fifth. He ruled more of Europe than anyone since Charlemagne.

He spent his whole life at war. Against France, against the Ottomans, against the Protestant princes of Germany. Exhausted, in 1556 he gave up his crowns and retired to a monastery.

His son, Philip the Second, inherited Spain and its empire. He ruled from a giant palace and monastery outside Madrid, the Escorial, where he read reports from every corner of the world. The Philippines are named after him.

In 1571, a Christian fleet led by Spain defeated the Ottoman navy at Lepanto. In 1580, Philip also became king of Portugal, and with it, Portugal's empire in Africa, Asia and Brazil.

Now Spain's possessions stretched across every time zone. Somewhere in its empire, the sun was always shining. It was the first empire on which the sun never set.

But in 1588, Philip sent a great fleet of around one hundred and thirty ships, the Armada, to invade Protestant England. English fire ships and terrible storms destroyed it. Only about half of the ships ever came home.

And there was a deeper problem. Despite all the silver from America, Philip's government went bankrupt again and again. Four times during his reign, it could not pay its debts. The silver came in, and went straight out again, to pay for war after war.

## Part 8: The Golden Age and the Long Decline

Strangely, as the empire began to weaken, Spanish culture reached its peak. Historians call it the Golden Age.

In 1605, Miguel de Cervantes published Don Quixote, the story of a poor old gentleman who reads so many books about knights that he decides to become one, and attacks windmills, thinking they are giants. It is often called the first modern novel.

In 1656, the painter Diego Velázquez painted Las Meninas, a picture of the little princess and her servants, with Velázquez himself standing at his canvas, looking straight out at us. Painters have puzzled over it ever since.

But the country itself was struggling. In 1609, the king ordered the expulsion of the Moriscos, the descendants of Spain's Muslims, who had been forced to become Christian. Around three hundred thousand people were driven out, many of them farmers and craftsmen.

Spain fought the Dutch for eighty years, and finally recognised their independence in 1648. Portugal broke away again in 1640. And France replaced Spain as the strongest power in Europe.

The Habsburg kings themselves were weakened by generations of marrying their own relatives. The last of them, Charles the Second, was sickly all his life, and his people called him the Bewitched.

When he died in 1700, he had no children. And the whole of Europe went to war over who would get Spain.

## Part 9: Bourbons and Napoleon

Charles had left his throne to a French prince, the grandson of King Louis the Fourteenth. Britain, Austria and the Dutch were terrified that France and Spain would become one superpower, and they went to war to stop it.

The War of the Spanish Succession lasted thirteen years. In the end, the French prince kept the throne as Philip the Fifth, the first Bourbon king of Spain. The Bourbons still sit on that throne today.

But the price was high. Spain lost its lands in Italy and the Netherlands. And Britain took Gibraltar, the rock where Tariq had landed a thousand years earlier. It is still British today.

In the war, Catalonia and Aragon had backed the losing side. On the eleventh of September 1714, after a long siege, Barcelona fell to Philip's army. Philip abolished the old laws and parliaments of Aragon, Catalonia and Valencia, and ruled them like Castile. Today, the eleventh of September is Catalonia's national day, and it is not a celebration of victory.

Unity versus difference. The key is turning again.

A century later, a new emperor came. In 1805, the British admiral Nelson destroyed the French and Spanish fleets at Trafalgar. And in 1808, Napoleon tricked the Spanish royal family into giving up the throne, and made his own brother, Joseph, king of Spain.

On the second of May, the people of Madrid rose up against the French. The revolt was crushed, and the next day, French soldiers shot prisoners outside the city. The painter Francisco Goya later painted that night, and it became one of the most famous paintings of war ever made.

All over Spain, ordinary people fought the French in small bands, with ambushes and raids. They called it the little war. In Spanish, guerrilla. The word went around the world.

In 1812, in the besieged city of Cádiz, a Spanish parliament wrote one of the first liberal constitutions in Europe. But when the war ended and King Ferdinand the Seventh came home, he tore it up and ruled as an absolute king.

## Part 10: Losing an Empire

While Spain was occupied by Napoleon, its colonies in America began to rule themselves. And they did not want to go back.

From 1810, led by men like Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín, the colonies fought for their independence. By 1825, almost all of Spanish America was free. Of its vast empire in the Americas, Spain kept only Cuba and Puerto Rico. In Asia, it still held the Philippines.

The rest of the nineteenth century in Spain was chaos. Kings were thrown out and brought back. Civil wars were fought between rival branches of the royal family. In 1873, Spain even became a republic. It lasted less than two years.

Then came the final blow. In 1898, Spain went to war with the United States over Cuba. In just a few months, Spain's fleets were destroyed, and it lost Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines.

Spaniards simply called it the Disaster. The empire on which the sun never set was gone.

## Part 11: The Two Spains

In the early twentieth century, Spain was a poor and deeply divided country. In the cities, workers joined unions and dreamed of revolution. In the countryside, millions of landless labourers worked the estates of rich landowners. The Catholic Church and the army were powerful, and suspicious of change.

Spaniards began to talk about two Spains. One was Catholic, traditional and conservative. The other was secular, liberal and revolutionary. And each saw the other as the enemy.

In 1923, a general named Miguel Primo de Rivera took power as dictator, with the king's approval. When the dictatorship collapsed, the king's reputation collapsed with it. In April 1931, after local elections, King Alfonso the Thirteenth left the country, and Spain became a republic once again.

The Second Republic tried to change everything at once. It separated church and state, reformed the army, began to break up big estates, gave women the right to vote, and granted self-government to Catalonia.

For some Spaniards, it was a dream. For others, it was a nightmare. Strikes, street fighting and political murders became common. Churches were burned. The country split into two hostile camps.

In February 1936, a coalition of the left, called the Popular Front, narrowly won the elections. And a group of army generals began to plan a coup.

## Part 12: The Civil War

On the seventeenth of July 1936, the army rose against the Republic. The coup succeeded in about half of Spain, and failed in the other half, including Madrid and Barcelona.

Spain was cut in two. And a civil war began.

One side was the Republic, supported by liberals, socialists, communists, anarchists, and the Basque and Catalan nationalists. The other side called themselves the Nationalists, supported by the army, the Church, the landowners and the monarchists. Their leader was a short, cautious general named Francisco Franco.

The war quickly became international. Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy sent planes, tanks and tens of thousands of soldiers to help Franco. The Soviet Union sent weapons to the Republic. And around thirty-five thousand volunteers came from more than fifty countries to fight for the Republic in the International Brigades.

On the twenty-sixth of April 1937, German and Italian planes bombed the small Basque town of Guernica on a market day, and much of it burned. Days later, Pablo Picasso began a huge black and white painting of the attack. Guernica became the most famous anti-war painting in the world.

Both sides carried out terrible killings behind their own lines. Priests, landowners and conservatives were murdered in the Republican zone. Workers, teachers and Republicans were shot in the Nationalist zone, often in far greater numbers.

The Nationalists had better weapons and more foreign help, while the Republic was torn by fighting among its own supporters. In March 1939, Madrid fell. On the first of April 1939, Franco declared the war over.

Historians estimate that around half a million people died. Nearly half a million more fled across the border into France.

## Part 13: Franco

Franco ruled Spain for the next thirty-six years, as dictator.

After the war, his regime took revenge. Tens of thousands of Republicans were executed, and hundreds of thousands were sent to prisons and labour camps. Political parties were banned. The Catalan and Basque languages were pushed out of public life. Spain went through years of hunger.

When the Second World War began a few months later, Franco met Hitler, but Spain stayed officially out of the war. It was too exhausted to fight. After Hitler's defeat, Franco's Spain was shunned by the world.

Then the Cold War rescued him. Franco was fiercely anti-communist, and in 1953, the United States signed a deal to build military bases in Spain. Two years later, Spain joined the United Nations.

In 1959, Franco's government opened up the economy. Foreign money flowed in. Millions of Spaniards moved from the countryside to the cities, or went abroad to work. And millions of tourists began to arrive at the beaches of the Mediterranean. In the 1960s, Spain's economy grew faster than almost any other in Europe. They called it the Spanish miracle.

But the old divisions had not gone away. In 1959, a Basque group called ETA was founded to fight for Basque independence. In 1973, ETA blew up the car of Franco's prime minister and chosen successor, Admiral Carrero Blanco, in the middle of Madrid.

On the twentieth of November 1975, Franco died. He had already chosen who would follow him. Not a general, but a young prince. Juan Carlos, the grandson of the last king.

## Part 14: Democracy

Almost everyone expected Juan Carlos to continue Franco's regime. Instead, he took it apart.

In 1976, the king chose a young politician named Adolfo Suárez as prime minister. Together, they convinced Franco's own parliament to vote itself out of existence. Political parties were legalised, even the Communist Party. And in June 1977, Spain held its first free elections in forty-one years.

In 1978, Spaniards approved a new constitution in a referendum. Spain became a democracy, a constitutional monarchy, with no official religion. And to answer the oldest question in its history, unity or difference, the constitution created something new. Seventeen autonomous communities, each with its own parliament. Catalonia and the Basque Country got wide self-government, and their languages became official alongside Spanish.

But not everyone accepted it. On the twenty-third of February 1981, a lieutenant colonel of the Civil Guard, Antonio Tejero, stormed into parliament with armed men and fired shots into the ceiling. Tanks rolled into the streets of Valencia. For one long night, the whole country held its breath.

After midnight, the king appeared on television in his military uniform and ordered the army to support democracy. The coup collapsed.

In 1982, the Socialists won the election, and power passed peacefully from one side to the other.

In 1986, Spain joined the European Community. And in 1992, Barcelona hosted the Olympic Games, and Seville hosted a World Expo, as the whole world watched a modern, confident Spain.

Do you remember that year from before? 1492. The fall of Granada, the expulsion of the Jews, and Columbus.

1992 was exactly five hundred years later. And Spain had chosen it to show the world who it had become.

## Part 15: Modern Spain

The good years continued. In 2002, Spain adopted the euro. Its economy boomed, and millions of immigrants came to work there.

But on the eleventh of March 2004, Islamist terrorists set off bombs on four commuter trains in Madrid. One hundred and ninety-three people were killed. It was the deadliest terrorist attack in Spain's history.

Then, in 2008, the housing bubble burst. Spain fell into a deep crisis. By 2013, more than a quarter of all workers had no job, and among young people, more than half. Hundreds of thousands of young Spaniards left to find work abroad.

There was some good news. In 2010, Spain won the football World Cup for the first time. And in 2011, after more than four decades and more than eight hundred deaths, ETA announced that it was giving up violence for good. In 2018, it dissolved itself.

But the oldest question came back once more. In Catalonia, support for independence grew. On the first of October 2017, the Catalan government held a referendum on independence, which Spain's highest court had declared illegal. Police tried to stop people from voting, and images of the clashes went around the world. Weeks later, the Catalan parliament declared independence. Madrid took direct control of the region, and Catalan leaders were put on trial or fled abroad.

Today, Spain is home to about forty-nine million people. Spanish, Catalan, Basque and Galician are all spoken within its borders. And Spanish, the language that grew from the Latin of Hispania, is now the native language of around five hundred million people around the world. More than ninety million foreign tourists visit Spain every year.

## The Ending

So let's go back to our questions.

How did a land conquered again and again become the most powerful empire on Earth? Because all those invaders left something behind. Roman roads and Latin, Muslim science and architecture, and centuries of war that made Castile a kingdom of soldiers. When the crowns joined in 1492, that energy turned outward, across the ocean.

Why did the richest empire in history keep going bankrupt? Because the silver of America paid for wars in Europe, and very little of it was used to build anything at home.

And how did a country that tore itself apart in a civil war become a democracy in one generation? Because Spaniards, on both sides, remembered that war, and decided never to repeat it.

Remember the key. Spain was never one thing. It was always many peoples and many kingdoms, sharing one peninsula.

From the Visigoths to the Catholic Monarchs, from Philip the Fifth to Franco, many rulers tried to force those differences into one shape. And every time, the differences survived. The Spain of today is the first that has tried to live with them instead.

Whether it can keep doing that is the next chapter. And it has not been written yet.
