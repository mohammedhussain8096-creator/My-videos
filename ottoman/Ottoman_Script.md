# The Entire History of the Ottoman Empire in 45 Minutes

## The Hook

Around the year 1280, on a dusty hillside in what is now western Turkey, a man named Osman sat outside his tent. He did not have a city. He did not have a palace. He had a small village called Söğüt, a few hundred horsemen, and a lot of sheep.

Honestly, if you had told the neighbours that this guy's family was about to build one of the greatest empires in history, they would have laughed you out of the valley.

But that is exactly what happened.

Osman's family would go on to rule for six hundred and twenty-three years. Thirty-six sultans, one single family, father to son, brother to brother, from the 1290s all the way to 1922.

At its peak, their empire stretched across three continents. From the gates of Vienna to the Persian Gulf. From the deserts of Algeria to the shores of the Caspian Sea. More than five million square kilometers. Dozens of different peoples. Christians, Muslims, Jews, Greeks, Arabs, Serbs, Armenians, Kurds, Hungarians and Turks, all ruled from one city.

And here is the crazy part. The Roman Empire in the west lasted about five hundred years. The Mongol Empire split apart within a few generations. The British Empire rose and shrank within a couple of centuries. The Ottomans outlasted almost all of them.

So how did a tribe of a few hundred horsemen become a superpower that terrified Europe for three hundred years? Why did a family that ruled the world end up locking its own princes in a cage? And how did it all disappear in just four years?

Here is the secret, and it runs through this whole story. The Ottomans had one superpower. They turned outsiders into insiders. Enemies became soldiers, slaves became generals, and conquered peoples became the empire. As long as that worked, nobody could stop them. The moment it stopped working, everything fell apart.

Let's go back to the very beginning.

## Part 1: The Man With a Dream

In the late 1200s, the land we now call Turkey was a mess. The old Byzantine Empire still held the coast and the city of Constantinople, but it was tired, broke and shrinking. The Seljuk Turks, who had ruled the interior, had just been smashed by the Mongols.

What was left was a patchwork of tiny Turkish warlord states called beyliks. Dozens of them. Each one with a chief, some horsemen, and big ambitions.

Osman's beylik was one of the smallest. It sat right on the border with the Byzantines. That sounds like a terrible place to live. Raids, ambushes, burned farms. But it turned out to be the best real estate in the world.

Because on the frontier, there was always someone to fight, and fighting brought loot, land and fame. Warriors from all over came to join the man on the border who was winning.

Later Ottoman storytellers said that Osman had a dream. He fell asleep in the house of a holy man and saw the moon rise out of the holy man's chest and sink into his own. Then a giant tree grew out of his body. Its branches covered the whole world, and under its shade flowed four great rivers.

The holy man woke up and said: congratulations, God has given the world to you and your children. Also, you can marry my daughter.

Historians are pretty sure this story was written much later, when the empire was already huge and needed a good origin story. But it tells you how the Ottomans saw themselves. Not as a lucky tribe. As a family chosen to rule the world.

Osman spent his life raiding Byzantine towns and picking off small forts. When he died around 1324, his little state had grown, but it was still small.

His son Orhan was the one who made it real.

## Part 2: The First Capital

In 1326, after a long siege, Orhan captured the Byzantine city of Bursa. It was rich, it had walls, and it had markets. For the first time, the Ottomans had a real capital.

And Orhan did something clever. He did not wipe out the Christians who lived there. He let them keep their churches, their homes and their trades, as long as they paid a special tax. A conquered city that keeps working pays far more than a burned one.

This would become the Ottoman formula. Conquer, then keep the people running things. Turn the conquered into taxpayers.

Then came the lucky break. In 1354, a powerful earthquake shook the Gallipoli peninsula, on the European side of the Dardanelles strait. The walls of the town of Gallipoli collapsed. The Byzantine defenders fled.

Orhan's son Süleyman Pasha simply walked in.

Byzantine messenger: Excuse me, that's our town.

Ottoman soldier: God sent the earthquake. We are just following instructions.

The Ottomans now had a foothold in Europe. And they were never going to leave.

## Part 3: The Secret Weapon

Orhan's son Murad the First turned the Ottomans from raiders into an army. He pushed deep into the Balkans and took the city of Adrianople, which the Turks renamed Edirne, and made it his new capital, right in the middle of Europe.

But Murad had a problem every growing empire has. His best warriors were Turkish nobles with their own land and their own ideas. If they got too powerful, they could turn on him.

So the Ottomans came up with one of the strangest systems in history.

It was called the devşirme, which means the collection. Every few years, Ottoman officials would travel to Christian villages in the Balkans and take a share of the boys, usually between eight and eighteen years old. The boys were taken from their families, converted to Islam, taught Turkish, and trained for years.

It was brutal. Families lost their sons. Some parents hid them. Some tried to marry them off early so they would be too old to take.

But here is what is strange. Those boys did not become ordinary slaves. They became the elite of the empire.

The strongest became the Janissaries, the sultan's personal infantry. They were among the first standing professional armies in Europe since Rome. They trained all year, they wore uniforms, they marched in silence, and they had no family, no land and no loyalty to anyone except the sultan.

The smartest boys went to the palace school. They learned languages, law, mathematics and poetry. Some of them rose to become governors, admirals and grand viziers, the most powerful men in the empire after the sultan himself.

Think about that. A shepherd boy from a Serbian village could end up running the largest empire in the world. In most of Europe at the time, if your father was a peasant, you were a peasant. Forever.

This is the Ottoman superpower in action. They took their enemies' children and turned them into the empire's most loyal servants.

In 1389, Murad's army met a Serbian-led coalition at the Battle of Kosovo. It was a bloody, close fight. Murad himself was killed, stabbed, according to legend, by a Serbian knight who pretended to surrender. But the Ottomans won the battle. Serbia would never again stop the Ottoman advance.

## Part 4: The Thunderbolt and the Cage

Murad's son Bayezid took the throne right there on the battlefield. And his first act set a terrible precedent. He had his own brother Yakub killed, so that nobody could challenge him.

Remember that. It is going to come back.

Bayezid was nicknamed Yıldırım, the Thunderbolt, because of how fast he moved his armies. In 1396, a huge crusader army of French, Hungarian and German knights marched down the Danube to throw the Turks out of Europe. At the Battle of Nicopolis, Bayezid crushed them. Thousands of knights were killed or captured.

Europe was terrified. Bayezid started besieging Constantinople. He seemed unstoppable.

Then he made a mistake. He picked a fight with someone even scarier.

His name was Timur, also known as Tamerlane, a Turco-Mongol conqueror from Central Asia who had already flattened Baghdad, Delhi and half of Persia. The two of them traded insulting letters for a while.

Bayezid: You are a dog from the steppes.

Timur: Interesting. I have a very, very large number of dogs.

In 1402, at the Battle of Ankara, Timur's army destroyed the Ottomans. Some of Bayezid's own troops switched sides in the middle of the battle. Bayezid was captured. Later stories said Timur kept him in an iron cage and used him as a footstool. That is probably exaggerated. But Bayezid did die in captivity a year later.

And the empire fell apart.

## Part 5: Brother Against Brother

With their father gone, Bayezid's sons went to war with each other. For eleven years, from 1402 to 1413, the Ottoman lands were split between rival princes, each backed by different nobles, cities and even Byzantine and Venetian allies.

Historians call it the Ottoman Interregnum. Basically, a family dinner that lasted a decade and ended in funerals.

The survivor was Mehmed the First. He reunited the empire, and his son Murad the Second spent thirty years rebuilding it.

Murad the Second was a strange sultan. He was a great general, but he seems to have been tired of power. In 1444, he did something almost no ruler ever does. He stepped down and handed the throne to his twelve-year-old son, Mehmed, so he could retire to a quiet life.

Europe saw its chance. A crusader army led by the young King of Hungary and Poland marched toward the Black Sea coast.

The boy sultan wrote to his father. According to the famous story, the letter said: if you are the sultan, come and lead your armies. If I am the sultan, I order you to come and lead my armies.

Murad came back. At the Battle of Varna in 1444, the crusaders were crushed, and the Hungarian king was killed. Then Murad went back into retirement, came back again a couple of years later, and ruled until he died.

That twelve-year-old boy would grow up to be one of the most famous rulers in history.

But the Ottomans had learned a terrible lesson from the civil war. A dead sultan with five living sons is a recipe for disaster. Remember that, too.

Because the next sultan was going to turn that lesson into law.

## Part 6: The Conqueror

In 1451, a nineteen-year-old named Mehmed the Second became sultan. He was intense, brilliant and impatient. He spoke several languages, read Greek and Persian, and was obsessed with Alexander the Great.

And he had one goal. Constantinople.

For eleven hundred years, the city had been the capital of the Roman Empire in the east. Its triple walls had stopped Huns, Persians, Arabs and the Ottomans themselves. It sat right in the middle of Ottoman territory, like a hole in the middle of a doughnut.

In the spring of 1453, Mehmed arrived with around eighty thousand men and something new. Giant bronze cannons, built by a Hungarian engineer named Orban, that could fire stone balls weighing more than half a tonne.

For fifty-three days, the cannons smashed the walls. When the Byzantines blocked the harbor with a huge iron chain, Mehmed's engineers built a road of greased logs over the hills and dragged seventy warships overland into the harbor behind the chain.

On the morning of May 29th, 1453, the Ottomans broke through. The last Roman emperor, Constantine the Eleventh, died fighting on the walls. Mehmed rode into the city, went to the great church of Hagia Sophia, and turned it into a mosque.

He was twenty-one years old.

From now on, he was known as Fatih, the Conqueror. And Constantinople, which the Turks would call Istanbul, became the Ottoman capital.

But Mehmed did something else that tells you exactly how his mind worked. He invited Greeks, Armenians and Jews to come and live in the half-empty city. He even appointed a new Greek Orthodox patriarch. He wanted his capital full, rich and working. Outsiders became insiders, again.

And then he wrote a law.

In his law code, Mehmed declared that whichever of his sons took the throne could legally execute his brothers, for the order of the world.

Read that again. The sultan's sons now knew that when their father died, one of them would become the most powerful man on earth, and all the others would die.

It sounds monstrous, and it was. But in the Ottoman logic, a few dead princes were better than another eleven-year civil war that killed thousands.

## Part 7: The Grim

Mehmed's grandson Selim the First took the throne in 1512 by overthrowing his own father. Then he killed his brothers and nephews. His nickname was Yavuz, which is usually translated as the Grim.

There was a joke among his officials. May you become Selim's vizier, was a common curse. Because his viziers kept getting executed.

But Selim reigned for only eight years, and in those eight years he more than doubled the size of the empire.

In 1514, at the Battle of Chaldiran, he crushed the Safavid Shah of Persia. The Ottoman cannons and Janissary muskets destroyed the Persian cavalry. This battle created a border between the Ottoman and Persian worlds that, roughly, still exists today, between Turkey and Iran.

Then Selim turned south, against the Mamluk Sultanate, the old power that ruled Egypt and Syria. In 1516 and 1517, he won two huge battles and marched into Cairo.

Suddenly, the Ottomans ruled Syria, Egypt, Jerusalem, and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina. The sultan took the title Servant of the Two Holy Sanctuaries. The Ottoman sultans would increasingly see themselves as the leaders of the whole Muslim world, the caliphs.

A family of frontier raiders now controlled the spice routes, the grain of the Nile and the holiest places in Islam.

And the next sultan was about to take it all to its peak.

## Part 8: The Magnificent

In 1520, Selim's only surviving son, Süleyman, became sultan. In Europe, they called him Süleyman the Magnificent. In the empire, they called him Kanuni, the Lawgiver.

Within six years, he took Belgrade, the key fortress on the Danube, captured the island of Rhodes from the Knights of Saint John, and in 1526 destroyed the Hungarian army at the Battle of Mohács in about two hours. The young King of Hungary drowned in a stream while fleeing.

In 1529, Süleyman reached the walls of Vienna, the heart of Habsburg Europe.

And here, for the first time, the Ottoman machine hit its limit. The heavy rains turned the roads into mud. The big siege cannons had to be left behind. The army had marched more than a thousand kilometers from Istanbul, and the campaign season was short. After a few weeks of failed assaults, Süleyman turned back.

But on the sea, the Ottomans were rising. A former pirate named Hayreddin Barbarossa became the admiral of the Ottoman fleet.

Barbarossa's story is wild even by Ottoman standards. He and his older brother Oruç grew up on the Greek island of Lesbos and became corsairs, raiding Christian ships across the Mediterranean. Oruç was famous for his red beard, and Europeans called him Barbarossa, Red Beard. When Oruç was killed, Hayreddin took over the business, and the nickname.

He captured the city of Algiers. And then, instead of keeping it as his own little pirate kingdom, he offered it to the Ottoman sultan. In return, he got soldiers, cannons and a title. A pirate became a governor, and later the grand admiral of the empire.

Once again, the Ottomans took an outsider and made him one of their own.

And Süleyman made an even more surprising friend. In 1525, the King of France, Francis the First, was defeated and captured by his great rival, the Habsburg emperor Charles the Fifth. From prison, the French royal family sent a desperate message to the one man who scared the Habsburgs most. The Ottoman sultan.

A Catholic king asking a Muslim sultan for help against another Catholic king. Europe was scandalized.

But it worked. France and the Ottomans became allies. In 1543, an Ottoman fleet under Barbarossa even spent the winter in the French port of Toulon. The French moved the townspeople out so that thousands of Ottoman sailors could move in.

Toulon resident: Where are we supposed to go?

French official: Anywhere. The Turks are our guests now.

Religion mattered. But in the end, power mattered more. In 1538, at the Battle of Preveza, he defeated a combined Christian fleet, and for decades the eastern Mediterranean was practically an Ottoman lake.

Under Süleyman, the empire reached its golden age. The great architect Mimar Sinan, himself a boy taken through the devşirme, built hundreds of buildings, including the giant Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul, which still dominates the skyline today.

Süleyman rewrote the empire's laws, published them, and tried to make them fair across regions. Istanbul became the biggest city in Europe, with maybe half a million people.

And then there was Hürrem. She was a slave girl from what is now Ukraine, captured in a raid and brought to the palace harem. Europeans called her Roxelana. Süleyman fell so hard for her that he did something no sultan had done in centuries. He freed her and married her.

Hürrem became one of the most powerful people in the empire. She wrote letters to foreign kings, built mosques and hospitals, and advised the sultan on politics.

But palace politics were deadly. In 1553, Süleyman had his eldest son Mustafa, a popular prince who was not Hürrem's son, strangled in his tent. Many at the time whispered that Hürrem and her allies had turned the sultan against him.

And even Süleyman's luck had limits. In 1565, his fleet besieged the island of Malta, held by the Knights of Saint John, the same knights he had kicked out of Rhodes. For four months, the Ottomans bombarded the forts. The knights held on, and the Ottomans sailed home.

A year later, Süleyman, now over seventy and sick with gout, insisted on leading one more campaign in Hungary. During the siege of the fortress of Szigetvár in 1566, he died in his tent.

And then his grand vizier did something extraordinary. He hid it. He was terrified that if the army found out, the soldiers would riot before the new sultan could take control. So for weeks, the sultan's death was kept secret. Orders were issued in his name. According to some accounts, a servant who looked like him was even placed in the royal carriage.

Grand vizier: The sultan is resting.

Soldier: He has been resting for a very long time.

Grand vizier: He is resting magnificently.

Only when his son Selim the Second was safely on his way to take the throne was the news announced. The greatest of all the sultans died the way he had lived, at war.

Again and again in this story, the empire's greatest strength, absolute loyalty to one man, turns into its deadliest weakness.

## Part 9: How the Empire Worked

Before we watch the long decline, let's look at how this giant thing actually worked. Because ruling dozens of peoples on three continents is not easy.

At the top was the sultan. Under him, the grand vizier ran the government. Most of the top officials were slaves of the sultan, products of the devşirme. That sounds terrible, but it meant they owned nothing, had no noble families and owed everything to him.

Then there was the millet system. Each religious community, Orthodox Christians, Armenians and Jews, was allowed to run its own affairs. Its own religious leaders, its own courts for marriage and inheritance, its own schools.

They had to pay extra taxes, and they were clearly second-class compared to Muslims. But compared to most of Europe at the time, where religious minorities were often expelled or burned, it was remarkably tolerant.

When Spain expelled its Jews in 1492, the Ottoman sultan Bayezid the Second welcomed many of them into his empire. There is a famous line attributed to him. The King of Spain is said to be wise, but he has made his own country poor and mine rich.

This is the superpower again. The empire did not try to make everyone the same. It made everyone useful.

What was it like to actually live in Ottoman Istanbul?

Picture the Grand Bazaar, started under Mehmed the Conqueror and growing into one of the largest covered markets in the world. Thousands of shops. Spices from India, silk from Persia, coffee from Yemen, furs from Russia, all under one roof.

Picture the hammams, the public bathhouses, where people went not just to get clean, but to gossip, relax and do business. And picture the palace kitchens at Topkapı, which cooked for thousands of people every single day, burning through mountains of rice, mutton, sugar and honey.

One of the best windows into this world is a man named Evliya Çelebi. In the 1600s, he spent about forty years travelling across the empire and beyond, and he wrote it all down in a ten-volume travel book. He describes cities, markets, mosques and street food from Bosnia to Egypt. He also exaggerates, a lot. But that is part of the fun.

He makes the empire sound like one giant, noisy, colorful world. And in a way, it was.

And then there was the palace. Topkapı, in Istanbul, was not just a home. It was the brain of the empire. Thousands of people lived inside. Pages, guards, cooks, scholars, and the harem, the private family quarters where the sultan's mother, wives, concubines and children lived.

The most powerful woman in the empire was usually not a wife. It was the Valide Sultan, the sultan's mother. In the 1600s, a series of sultans' mothers became so powerful that historians call this period the Sultanate of Women.

The most famous of them was Kösem Sultan. She was a Greek girl who entered the harem as a teenager and became the favorite of Sultan Ahmed the First. Two of her sons became sultans, and for decades she effectively ran the empire through them. She chose viziers, negotiated with the Janissaries and controlled huge fortunes.

She held power under four sultans: her husband, two of her sons, and her grandson. But in 1651, during a struggle for power with her grandson's mother, she was strangled inside the palace. The most powerful woman in the empire was killed in the same harem where she had started as a slave.

## Part 10: The Cage

Now remember the law of fratricide.

In 1595, when Mehmed the Third became sultan, he had nineteen of his brothers executed on his first day. Some of them were children. The people of Istanbul watched nineteen small coffins carried out of the palace, and they were horrified.

So, in the early 1600s, the Ottomans tried something new. Instead of killing the princes, they would lock them up.

The princes were kept in a part of the harem known as the kafes, the cage. Not an actual metal cage, but a set of rooms inside the palace that they could not leave. They lived there for years, sometimes decades, with no education in government, no experience of war, and the constant fear that the next knock on the door would be the executioner.

Then, when the sultan died, one of these men was suddenly pulled out of the cage and placed on the throne.

Palace official: Congratulations, you are now ruler of the world.

New sultan: I have not been outside in twenty-two years.

Some of them were fine. Some were clearly broken. One sultan, Ibrahim, spent so long in the cage that he was later known as Ibrahim the Mad.

His older brother, Murad the Fourth, was the opposite. He was tough, strict and terrifying. He banned coffee, tobacco and alcohol across the empire, because he believed the coffeehouses were breeding rebellion. Then, according to the stories, he walked the streets of Istanbul at night in disguise, looking for people breaking his ban. Some of those he caught were executed on the spot.

Imagine being caught with a cup of coffee by the sultan himself.

Murad the Fourth also led his armies into battle and took Baghdad back from Persia in 1638. But he died young, and the throne passed to his brother from the cage.

The old system had produced experienced, battle-hardened princes who fought their way to the throne. The new system produced men who had never seen a battlefield. Real power drifted to grand viziers, palace women and the Janissaries.

## Part 11: The Turning Points

The decline did not happen in one day. In fact, for a long time, it barely looked like a decline at all.

In 1571, a Christian alliance called the Holy League destroyed the Ottoman fleet at the Battle of Lepanto. Europe celebrated like the Turks were finished.

The Ottoman grand vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, another devşirme boy from Bosnia, told the Venetian ambassador: by taking Cyprus from you, we cut off your arm. By defeating our fleet, you only shaved our beard. A cut arm will not grow back. A shaved beard grows back thicker.

And he was right. Within a year, the Ottomans had rebuilt their entire fleet.

But something was slowly changing. Europe was getting richer. Spain and Portugal were bringing silver from the Americas and sailing around Africa to trade directly with India, cutting out the old routes through Ottoman lands. European states were building new kinds of armies, with better guns and new tactics.

And the Janissaries, once the most disciplined soldiers in Europe, were changing too. They were allowed to marry, to have businesses, to pass their positions to their sons. They became less of an army and more of a powerful club, and they rioted, again and again, whenever the sultan did something they did not like. Several sultans were overthrown or killed by their own soldiers.

Then came 1683.

A grand vizier named Kara Mustafa Pasha marched a huge army to Vienna once more. For two months, the Ottomans besieged the city and nearly broke through.

Then, on September 12th, 1683, a relief army arrived. Its leader was the Polish king, Jan Sobieski. At the head of the charge were the Polish winged hussars, heavy cavalry with giant feathered wings on their backs. It was one of the largest cavalry charges in history.

The Ottoman army collapsed. Kara Mustafa fled, and on the sultan's orders, he was executed in Belgrade a few months later.

Sixteen years later, in 1699, the Ottomans signed the Treaty of Karlowitz and lost most of Hungary. For the first time, the empire had permanently lost a big chunk of territory to Christian Europe.

The age of Ottoman expansion was over.

But before that disaster, there was one last comeback, and it was led by a very old man.

In 1656, the empire was in chaos. Rebellions, unpaid soldiers, a navy blockaded by the Venetians. In desperation, the sultan's mother appointed a grand vizier who was already somewhere around eighty years old. His name was Köprülü Mehmed Pasha.

He had one condition. Total power. No interference from the palace.

And then he purged the system. Thousands of officials and soldiers he considered disloyal or corrupt were removed, and many were executed. It was ruthless. But it worked. His son took over after him, and the Köprülü family of viziers brought the empire back to strength. They finally captured the island of Crete in 1669, after a siege of the city of Candia that had lasted twenty-one years.

Twenty-one years. Some of the soldiers in that siege had been born after it started.

Under the Köprülüs, the empire reached its largest size in Europe. It was the high point. And it was immediately followed by the fall.

## Part 12: Tulips and Coffee

Here is something most people do not know. The 1700s were not all bad news for the Ottomans.

From 1718 to 1730, there was a period of peace and luxury that historians call the Tulip Era. Tulips originally came from Central Asia and were adored by the Ottomans long before the Dutch went crazy for them. The elite of Istanbul threw garden parties at night, with tortoises walking through the tulip beds carrying candles on their shells.

At least, that is how visitors at the time described it.

The Ottomans also gave Europe one of its favorite addictions. Coffee. Coffeehouses spread across Istanbul in the 1500s, and they became places to talk, gossip, play games and criticize the government. Several sultans tried to ban them. It never worked.

The first printing press for Turkish books also opened in this period. The empire was curious and open.

But the party ended in 1730 with a rebellion led by a Janissary named Patrona Halil. The sultan was forced to give up his throne, and the grand vizier was killed.

## Part 13: The Bear From the North

Meanwhile, a new enemy was rising. Russia.

Actually, there were two. In 1798, a French general named Napoleon Bonaparte suddenly landed in Egypt with an army, beat the local Mamluk forces at the Battle of the Pyramids and marched into Cairo. Egypt was officially Ottoman land. The Ottomans had to team up with the British to push the French out, which they finally did in 1801.

It was a shock. A European army had walked into the heart of the Islamic world, and the Ottomans could not stop it alone.

Under Peter the Great and then Catherine the Great, Russia pushed south toward the Black Sea, again and again. In 1774, after a disastrous war, the Ottomans signed the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca. They lost control of Crimea, and Russia gained the right to sail its ships through Ottoman waters. A few years later, Russia simply annexed Crimea.

The Black Sea, once an Ottoman lake, was now shared with the bear.

Ottoman officials could see what was happening. They were falling behind. So a young sultan, Selim the Third, tried to build a modern army trained in the European style. He called it the New Order.

The Janissaries hated it. A modern army would make them useless. In 1807, they rebelled, overthrew Selim and later killed him.

His cousin, Mahmud the Second, waited almost twenty years for his revenge.

In 1826, he announced a new modern army again. When the Janissaries rioted, he was ready. Loyal artillery shelled the Janissary barracks in Istanbul. Thousands were killed, and the corps was abolished. Ottoman history calls this the Auspicious Incident.

After nearly five hundred years, the most famous army in the empire's history was gone in a single day.

## Part 14: The Sick Man of Europe

The 1800s were the century of nationalism. And nationalism was poison for an empire made of forty peoples.

The Serbs revolted. Then the Greeks, who won independence by 1830 with help from Britain, France and Russia. Egypt, under its ambitious governor Muhammad Ali, became practically independent and nearly marched on Istanbul.

Muhammad Ali was an Albanian officer sent to Egypt to fight the French. He stayed, took power, and in 1811 he invited the leading Mamluk commanders to a grand ceremony at the Cairo Citadel. When they rode down a narrow passage on the way out, the gates were closed and his soldiers opened fire. The Mamluks, who had dominated Egypt for more than five hundred years, were wiped out in an afternoon.

Then he built a modern army and navy, factories and schools, and by the 1830s his son was beating Ottoman armies in Syria. The sultan's own governor had become his most dangerous rival.

The old Ottoman superpower, turning outsiders into insiders, was failing. People no longer wanted to be useful members of a great multinational empire. They wanted their own countries.

So the Ottomans tried to reinvent themselves. In 1839, they announced a series of reforms called the Tanzimat, the reorganization. The sultan promised security and the same rights to all subjects, Muslim or not. New schools, new courts, a new army, telegraph lines, railways and uniforms. Sultans swapped turbans for the fez and European-style jackets.

The Russian Tsar Nicholas the First is said to have called the Ottoman Empire the sick man of Europe. Everyone assumed it would soon die, and the great powers argued about who would get the pieces.

In the Crimean War of 1853 to 1856, Britain and France actually fought alongside the Ottomans against Russia, not out of love, but to stop Russia from getting too strong.

And to pay for the war, the Ottomans did something new. They borrowed money from European banks. Then they borrowed more. Then more, to pay the interest on the earlier loans.

By 1875, the empire could not pay. It went bankrupt. A few years later, European creditors set up the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, which took direct control of important taxes inside the empire.

The empire that once collected tribute from half of Europe was now handing its tax money to European bankers.

## Part 15: The Last Strong Sultan

In 1876, a new sultan, Abdul Hamid the Second, agreed to the empire's first constitution and an elected parliament. Then, two years later, during another disastrous war with Russia, he shut the parliament down and ruled alone for thirty years.

He was paranoid, secretive, and ran a huge network of spies. But he was also a moderniser. He built railways, including one toward Mecca, schools and telegraph lines.

And he leaned hard on one idea. If the empire could no longer hold people together by being useful, maybe it could hold them together by religion. He presented himself to the world as the caliph of all Muslims.

But many of the empire's educated young officers had a different idea. They wanted a modern constitutional state. They were called the Young Turks.

In 1908, they forced Abdul Hamid to bring back the constitution, and a year later they removed him from the throne.

Then the empire lost almost everything it still held in Europe. In the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913, Serbia, Greece, Bulgaria and Montenegro attacked together. Within months, Ottoman Europe, which had lasted more than five hundred years, was nearly gone.

The Ottomans were now an empire of Anatolia and the Arab lands, run by a small group of Young Turk leaders.

And then they made their final bet.

## Part 16: The Final War

In 1914, the First World War broke out. The Ottoman leaders joined on the side of Germany.

At first, there was a stunning victory. In 1915, Britain and France tried to force their way through the Dardanelles and capture Istanbul. The Gallipoli campaign became a bloody disaster for the Allies. After months of fighting, they withdrew. One of the Ottoman commanders who made his name there was a colonel named Mustafa Kemal.

But the war also brought one of the darkest chapters in this entire story.

In 1915, the Ottoman government began the mass deportation of Armenians from Anatolia, accusing them of siding with Russia. Families were marched into the Syrian desert. Many were massacred. Many died of hunger, thirst and disease on the way.

Historians estimate that somewhere between hundreds of thousands and more than a million Armenians died. Most historians and many countries call it a genocide. The Turkish state disputes that term to this day.

Other Christian communities, including Assyrians and Greeks, also suffered terribly during these years.

Meanwhile, in the south, Arab leaders backed by Britain launched the Arab Revolt against Ottoman rule. And in secret, Britain and France signed the Sykes-Picot Agreement, drawing lines on a map to divide the Ottoman Arab lands between themselves.

In October 1918, the Ottomans signed an armistice. They had lost. British and French troops occupied Istanbul.

## Part 17: The End of the Empire

In 1920, the Allies forced the sultan's government to sign the Treaty of Sèvres. It would have carved up even Anatolia, the Turkish heartland, between Greece, Armenia, France, Italy and Britain, leaving only a small Turkish state around Ankara.

The sultan's government signed it. The Turkish nationalists refused.

Led by Mustafa Kemal, the hero of Gallipoli, they built a new government and a new army in Ankara and fought what Turks call the War of Independence. They drove out the Greek army and forced the Allies to negotiate again.

And now, Mustafa Kemal did something that would have been unthinkable a few years earlier.

On November 1st, 1922, the new government abolished the sultanate.

A few weeks later, the last Ottoman sultan, Mehmed the Sixth, quietly slipped out of the palace, boarded a British warship, and sailed away into exile. He never came back.

In 1923, the Republic of Turkey was declared, with Ankara as its capital and Mustafa Kemal, later known as Atatürk, as its first president. In 1924, even the caliphate was abolished, and the members of the Ottoman family were sent abroad.

Six hundred and twenty-three years after a few hundred horsemen rode out of Söğüt, the House of Osman was gone.

## The Payoff

So, let's go back to our question. How did a tiny tribe become one of the greatest empires in history, and why did it fall?

The answer is that superpower. The Ottomans turned outsiders into insiders.

They took the conquered and made them taxpayers. They took Christian boys and made them generals and grand viziers. They took Jews expelled from Spain and Greeks, Armenians and Arabs and let them run their own communities. For centuries, almost anyone could become useful to the empire, and the empire grew.

But the same system had a dark side. Everything depended on loyalty to one man and one family. Brothers were killed. Princes were locked in cages. The army that served the sultan learned it could overthrow him.

And when the modern world arrived, with nationalism, factories, railways and banks, people no longer wanted to be useful parts of someone else's empire. They wanted to be nations of their own. The trick that built the empire stopped working.

And yet, look at the map today. The borders of Turkey, Iraq, Syria, Israel, Lebanon, Jordan and the Balkans were all drawn on Ottoman ruins. Istanbul is still one of the greatest cities on Earth. People across Europe and the Middle East still drink Ottoman coffee, eat Ottoman sweets and argue about Ottoman history.

The empire is gone. But the world it built is still all around us.

Not bad for a guy with a few hundred horsemen and a lot of sheep.
