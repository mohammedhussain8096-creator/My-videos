# The Gold the Nazis Couldn't See (script)

Based on the most-viewed clever-trick Short among the owner's reference channels:
**"The Jar That Hid Two Nobel Prizes"** (Segment Zero, about 5.5M views). Same true story, our own words,
written to The Clever Gap formula (paradox hook, "Then," turn, "You see," explainer with a household simile,
callback ending). 182 words, about 55 seconds.

## Hook options
1. The Nazis searched the whole lab for gold, and stared right at it. (used below)
2. The safest place to hide gold from the Nazis was a jar of acid.
3. Two Nobel Prizes sat on a shelf for five years, and nobody could see them.

## Script

The Nazis searched the whole lab for gold, and stared right at it. Copenhagen, 1940. German tanks rolled
into the city. Inside one lab sat two gold Nobel Prizes, owned by German scientists who had defied Hitler.
Their names were engraved on the front. If soldiers found them, both men were finished. Bury them? Soldiers
dig. Hide them in a wall? Soldiers smash walls. Then, a chemist named George de Hevesy grabbed a bottle of
acid. Aqua regia, one of the few liquids that can eat gold. He dropped both medals in, and watched them
disappear. All that was left was an orange liquid, in a jar, on a shelf. The soldiers came. They opened every
drawer. They looked right at the jar, and walked away. You see, the gold never left. It just stopped looking
like gold. Like sugar in tea: it's still in there, you just can't see it. After the war, Hevesy pulled the
gold back out, and it was recast into brand new medals. The Nazis were hunting for gold. It sat on a shelf
the whole time.

## Fact notes
- True: in April 1940, when Germany occupied Denmark, George de Hevesy dissolved the Nobel medals of
  Max von Laue and James Franck in aqua regia at Niels Bohr's institute in Copenhagen. The jar stayed there
  through the war; afterwards the gold was recovered and the medals were recast (Franck received his in 1952).
- Dramatised: soldiers searching the building and "looking right at the jar" is the popular telling, not a
  documented scene.
- Sources: NPR (Krulwich, 2011), nobelprize.org medal page, Mental Floss.
